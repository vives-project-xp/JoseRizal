When José Rizal sought to publish his second novel *El Filibusterismo* in 1891, he turned to a modest but capable printing press in the quiet Belgian city of Ghent: De Meyer-Van Loo, also known in historical records as F. Meyer-Van Loo. Though little known outside of Belgian publishing circles, this company would play a significant role in Philippine history by printing one of its most politically charged and enduring literary works.

## A Modest Press with a Revolutionary Impact

Located at 20 Heilige Geeststraat (Holy Ghost Street) in Ghent, F. Meyer-Van Loo was a typical 19th-century European printing house—family-run, artisanal in approach, and involved in publishing books, pamphlets, and academic material. During this time, Ghent had a thriving printing and publishing industry, owing to its status as a university town and commercial center.

Rizal chose this particular press not only for its affordability—Ghent was significantly cheaper than cities like Paris or Madrid—but also for its professionalism. He was working under extreme financial constraints, and *El Filibusterismo* was a risky publication: subversive, critical of the Spanish clergy and government, and likely to be banned.

Despite limited funds, the Meyer-Van Loo press agreed to take on the project. However, financial difficulties almost halted the printing midway. Rizal struggled to pay for the services, and printing was suspended until his friend Valentin Ventura stepped in to cover the remaining costs.

## Quality and Secrecy

Despite being a small operation, De Meyer-Van Loo printed *El Filibusterismo* with great care. The finished product was a relatively slim volume with a dark brown cover—symbolic of the somber and serious tone of the novel. Unlike his earlier work *Noli Me Tangere*, which carried hints of romanticism and hope, *El Filibusterismo* was sharp, bleak, and radical.

Because the book was politically sensitive, there was an effort to keep its production quiet. Any exposure to the Spanish authorities could have led to censorship or confiscation, not just of Rizal’s work but also of the printing press itself.

## A Quiet Legacy

Today, the original site of De Meyer-Van Loo in Ghent no longer functions as a printing house. The street itself has changed over time, and little physical evidence remains of the press that once helped publish one of the most influential novels in Southeast Asian history. Yet, the legacy of this small European printer lives on in libraries, museums, and classrooms across the Philippines.

Through their craftsmanship and willingness to work with a financially struggling but brilliant author, the team at De Meyer-Van Loo unwittingly became part of a colonial subject's revolution—printing a book that would stir a people to demand justice and independence.
